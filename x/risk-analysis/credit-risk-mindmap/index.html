The 5 C's of Credit
👤
Character
Trustworthiness & credit history
→ FICO score, bureau data
Example: A borrower with a 750 FICO and no prior delinquencies shows strong character. A 580 FICO with two prior collections raises flags.
💪
Capacity
Ability to repay
→ PTI ratio, income verification
Example: Borrower earns $4,000/mo with a $600 car payment → PTI = 15%. Manageable. At $1,000/mo payment → PTI = 25%. Risky.
🏦
Capital
Borrower's own investment
→ Down payment, lowers LTV
Example: $5K down on a $30K truck → 83% LTV. $0 down → 100% LTV. More capital = more skin in the game.
🚗
Collateral
Security for the loan
→ The vehicle itself
Example: A new Chevy Silverado holds value well. A 7-year-old sedan with 120K miles depreciates fast → weaker collateral.
📋
Conditions
Loan purpose & environment
→ Rates, economy, term
Example: An 84-month loan in a rising-rate environment with high unemployment = riskier conditions than a 48-month loan in a stable economy.
Leading vs. Lagging Indicators (Critical Concept)

⚡ Leading Indicators

  • 📊 Delinquency rates (30+ DPD)
  • 📈 Roll rates (30→60, 60→90)
  • 🔍 Early payment defaults
Why they matter: These signal trouble BEFORE losses hit. You can still act — adjust collections, tighten policy, increase reserves. Think of these as the "check engine light."
→

🕐 Lagging Indicators

  • 💸 Net charge-off rate
  • 📉 Loss severity (realized)
  • 📋 Recovery rates
Why they matter: These confirm damage already done. Useful for evaluating past underwriting decisions and validating models, but you can't prevent these losses — they're already on the books.
Key Credit Risk Metrics
Quality
FICO Score
Range: 300–850
Primary credit quality indicator. GM Financial uses in 90%+ of decisions. Tiers: Super-Prime (750+), Prime (720–749), Near-Prime (660–719), Subprime (<660).
Example GMF originates 10,000 loans in Q1. The weighted-average FICO is 695 (near-prime). If Q2's average drops to 670, it signals that credit policy may have loosened — underwriting is approving riskier borrowers. You'd flag this and check if approval criteria changed.
Risk
LTV (Loan-to-Value)
= Loan Amount ÷ Vehicle Value
Higher LTV = more risk. LTV > 100% means negative equity (borrower owes more than vehicle is worth).
Example A borrower finances $35,000 on a truck worth $30,000 (rolled in negative equity from their trade-in). LTV = $35,000 ÷ $30,000 = 117%
⚠️ If this borrower defaults in Month 6 and the truck is now worth $25K, GMF loses the full gap: $35K balance − $25K recovery = $10K loss.
Risk
PTI (Payment-to-Income)
= Monthly Payment ÷ Gross Monthly Income
Measures affordability. Higher PTI = more strain on borrower. Key underwriting metric.
Example Borrower earns $4,500/mo gross. Monthly car payment is $650. PTI = $650 ÷ $4,500 = 14.4% Comfortable. But if insurance rises by $150/mo, effective burden jumps even though PTI on paper stays the same. That's affordability compression — a hidden risk.
Leading
Delinquency Rate (DPD)
= Count(DPD ≥ 30) ÷ Total Accounts
Buckets: 30, 60, 90+ DPD. Early warning signal. Monitor by region, dealer, credit tier, vintage.
Example GMF portfolio has 500,000 active loans. In January, 20,000 are 30+ DPD. Delinquency rate = 20,000 ÷ 500,000 = 4.0% If it was 3.2% three months ago, the trend is concerning — even though 4% might sound low on its own. Always watch the trend, not just the level.
Leading
Roll Rate
= Accounts moving 30→60 DPD ÷ Accounts at 30 DPD
% of accounts worsening from one delinquency bucket to the next. Rising roll rates = deteriorating portfolio.
Example In March, 20,000 accounts are 30 DPD. By April, 8,000 of those same accounts are now 60 DPD. 30→60 roll rate = 8,000 ÷ 20,000 = 40% If last year's average was 30%, this means borrowers are self-curing at a lower rate. Expect higher charge-offs in 3–6 months. Time to ramp collections capacity and potentially repo vendor staffing.
Lagging
Net Charge-Off Rate
= (Gross Losses − Recoveries) ÷ Avg Receivables
Realized losses after recoveries. The bottom line on credit performance. Backward-looking — by the time this rises, the damage is done.
Example In Q3, GMF writes off $200M in defaulted loans. They recover $80M through auctions. Average portfolio balance is $50B. NCO = ($200M − $80M) ÷ $50B = 0.24% (quarterly) → ~0.96% annualized
This is the "final score." It tells you how underwriting decisions from 12–24 months ago actually performed. Useful for model validation and benchmarking, but not for early intervention.
Lagging
Loss Severity
= Loss Amount ÷ Balance at Default
How much is lost per defaulted account. Affected by vehicle depreciation, repossession timing, and auction recovery.
Example Borrower defaults with a $28,000 balance. The vehicle is repossessed and sold at auction for $18,000. Repo and auction costs total $2,000. Net recovery = $18,000 − $2,000 = $16,000 Loss = $28,000 − $16,000 = $12,000 Loss severity = $12,000 ÷ $28,000 = 42.9%
If used car prices drop 15% industry-wide, that auction recovery falls — and severity rises across the whole portfolio, even if borrower behavior doesn't change. That's normalization risk.
Volume
Approval Rate
= Approved Apps ÷ Total Apps
Indicates credit policy stringency. Dropping approval rate = tighter policy.
Example GMF receives 80,000 applications in Q1 and approves 52,000. Approval rate = 52,000 ÷ 80,000 = 65% If last quarter was 72%, it means policy tightened. Follow-up question: did the tightening come from raising the FICO floor, lowering max LTV, or reducing max term? Segmentation tells you.
Volume
Penetration Rate
= GMF-Financed Sales ÷ Total GM Sales
GM Financial's share of GM vehicle sales. Key business KPI that shows the captive lender's importance to the parent company.
Example GM sells 600,000 vehicles in a quarter. GMF finances 234,000 of them. Penetration = 234,000 ÷ 600,000 = 39%
This is why "just tighten credit" isn't always the right answer. Tighter credit → fewer approvals → lower penetration → weaker dealer relationships → GM sells fewer cars. Risk analysts must quantify the trade-off.
Lease
Residual Value Risk
= Projected Value − Actual Value at Lease End
Risk that vehicle is worth less than projected at lease return. High uncertainty for EVs due to battery degradation and rapid tech changes.
Example GMF writes a 36-month lease on a Chevy Equinox EV, projecting it'll be worth $22,000 at return. But battery technology improves rapidly and a newer model undercuts the used market. Actual value at return: $16,000. Residual loss = $22,000 − $16,000 = $6,000 per vehicle
Multiply by thousands of leases and this becomes a massive exposure. That's why EV residual value is one of the biggest emerging risks in auto finance.
Loan vs. Lease — Credit Risk Comparison

🔑 Loan (Retail Installment)

  • Ownership Customer buys the vehicle outright
  • Equity Builds equity over time as balance decreases
  • Primary Risk Borrower default + negative equity (balance > value)
  • Loss Driver Vehicle depreciation vs. loan paydown speed
  • Key Metrics LTV, DPD, charge-off rate, loss severity
  • Term Typically 48–84 months
Example scenario: A borrower takes an 84-month loan on a $40K SUV with $0 down (100% LTV). In Month 18, they've paid down to $35K — but the SUV is now worth $28K due to depreciation. They're $7K underwater. If they default now, GMF repossesses and sells at auction for ~$25K (after costs). Loss: ~$10K. The long term and zero down payment created this negative equity trap.

🔄 Lease

  • Ownership GM Financial retains ownership
  • Payments Customer pays for depreciation over term
  • Primary Risk Default + residual value risk at lease end
  • Extra Risks Early termination, excess mileage, excess wear
  • Key Metrics Residual value performance, turn-in rates, DPD
  • Term Typically 24–36 months
Example scenario: GMF leases 50,000 Chevy Blazers at a projected residual of $25K each. Three years later, market shifts push actual values to $21K. That's a $4K loss × 50,000 vehicles = $200M in residual value losses — and the borrower didn't even default. This is pure collateral risk, unique to leasing.
Vintage Analysis

📈 How It Works

What:
Group loans by origination period, track cumulative performance over time
Why:
Isolates the effect of credit policy changes and economic conditions at origination
Use case:
Did tightening the FICO cutoff in Q3 2024 improve that cohort's performance?
Without it:
You'd mix old and new originations, masking whether policy changes actually worked

🎓 Your Bridge: Cohort Analysis = Vintage Analysis
In education, you track each incoming class (cohort) over time: retention at Year 1, graduation at Year 4.
In lending, you track each origination period (vintage) over time: delinquency at Month 12, losses at Month 24.
Same framework: Define group by entry date → measure outcomes at consistent intervals → compare groups to isolate what changed.
Credit Policy Trade-Off

🔒 Tighten Policy (e.g., raise min FICO)

  • 📉 Volume: fewer applicants qualify, lower originations
  • 📈 Quality: higher avg FICO, lower delinquencies over time
  • 💰 Revenue: less volume = less interest income
  • 🤝 Dealers: may weaken relationships, hurt GM sales penetration
  • 📊 Vintages: newer cohorts should perform better
Example: GMF raises the minimum FICO from 620 to 660. This eliminates ~12% of applicants. Delinquency rates on new originations improve by 0.8% within 12 months — but dealer complaints increase and penetration drops from 39% to 35%.
⇄

🔓 Loosen Policy (e.g., lower min FICO)

  • 📈 Volume: more applicants qualify, higher originations
  • 📉 Quality: lower avg FICO, potentially higher delinquencies
  • 💰 Revenue: more volume = more interest income (higher rates too)
  • 🤝 Dealers: stronger relationships, higher GM penetration
  • 📊 Vintages: newer cohorts may underperform
Example: GMF lowers the FICO floor to 580 and raises max LTV to 120%. Volume jumps 18% and dealers are happy — but 12 months later, the 30+ DPD rate on that vintage is 6.2% vs. the portfolio average of 3.8%. The yield on those loans needs to cover the extra losses, or it was a bad trade-off.
